1 Introduction

≈369 words

1 Introduction

Every school day, many students in Dubai drink from a bottle. Some buy single-use bottled water, usually packaged in PET (polyethylene terephthalate), and others carry a reusable bottle, which in my school is often made from stainless steel. Reusable bottles are marketed as the eco-friendly option1. However, making a stainless-steel bottle needs far more material and energy than making a single PET bottle. One peer-reviewed life-cycle study gives about 1.02 kg CO2e to produce a stainless-steel bottle and about 0.09 kg CO2e to produce a PET bottle (Summa et al., 2025). A reusable bottle starts with a "carbon debt" that it can only repay by replacing single-use bottles many times. Whether the stainless-steel bottle is better therefore depends on how it is used.

This essay investigates the research question: To what extent is a reusable stainless-steel bottle a more sustainable choice than a single-use rPET bottle for students at a school in Dubai?2 The term rPET means PET that contains recycled material. I could not find a published footprint for a recycled-content bottle, so I use data for a typical PET bottle and discuss the effect of recycled content in the evaluation.

The topic belongs in Design Technology because a drinking bottle is a product whose material, manufacturing process, use and end of life are all design decisions. Product analysis and life-cycle thinking are the tools designers use to compare such decisions. To keep the question focused, I define "sustainable" as having a lower global warming potential over 100 years (GWP100), measured in kilograms of carbon dioxide equivalent (kg CO2e). This is the indicator reported by most of my sources. Other impacts, such as water use and toxicity, are discussed only briefly.3

The scope is one 500 mL bottle used by one student at one school, filled from school water coolers. I do not compare tap and bottled water, and I do not study glass or aluminium bottles. My interest comes from seeing classmates carry both kinds of bottle and from the school canteen selling bottled water. This essay is separate from my design project, which is about a different product and uses different data and sources.4

1Knowledge and understanding · What could be improved

"Eco-friendly" is a marketing phrase, not a technical term. Replace it with the measurable idea you actually use, for example "lower global warming potential".

2Focus and method · What went well

A clear, focused, answerable question. It names the product, the comparison and the user group. "To what extent" invites a reasoned judgement, not a yes/no.

3Focus and method · What went well

Narrowing "sustainable" to GWP100 in kg CO2e gives the essay a measurable focus. The student also says what is left out, which protects the 4,000 words.

4Focus and method · Check against the current rules

The guide says an EE must not overlap with the design project and may not reuse its materials or data. A short, specific statement like this is good practice. Your supervisor should confirm it.

2 Methodology

≈414 words

2 Methodology

2.1 Overall approach. I used the investigation and evaluation stages of the design cycle. A physical test of a bottle over hundreds of uses was not possible in the time available, so I combined two kinds of evidence. First, I used published life-cycle assessment (LCA) data for the footprint of making each bottle. Second, I collected primary data on how students at my school actually use their bottles, because behaviour decides how many times a bottle is refilled, washed or lost. The model can then use local inputs rather than only figures from other countries.1

2.2 Secondary sources. I chose sources that gave numerical results for PET or stainless-steel bottles and were published in 2017 or later. They are two peer-reviewed LCA studies (Summa et al., 2025; Olatayo et al., 2021), a university working paper (Diaz-Loar, 2024), a university sustainability office page (University of Wisconsin-Madison, 2017), an online summary of PET bottle impacts (NEMS, n.d.), a newspaper-style science article (Thornton and Lockrey, 2018) and two websites (Berical, 2025; KeepCup, 2026).2

2.3 Functional unit and system boundary. An LCA compares products that do the same job. My functional unit is "providing one 500 mL drink of water in a bottle to one student on one occasion at school". The system boundary includes making the bottle and washing the reusable bottle. It excludes the water itself, because both bottles would carry the same water, and it excludes transport and end of life, because I could not find consistent data for them. The effect of these exclusions is discussed in section 6.

2.4 The break-even model. If the reusable bottle has a production footprint Pr, a washing footprint w per use, and the single-use bottle has a production footprint p, then after n uses the reusable bottle has emitted Pr + nw and the single-use bottles have emitted np. The break-even point is n = Pr / (p − w). The reusable bottle is the lower-footprint choice from the next whole use onward. I then change one input at a time to see how much the result moves.

2.5 Primary data. I surveyed 40 students from two Grade 11 classes using a short paper questionnaire with six questions on bottle ownership, refilling, washing and loss. Responses were anonymous and students agreed to take part. I also analysed three 500 mL bottles (Bottle A, B and C): I weighed each on a kitchen scale (±1 g) and recorded the material and shop price.34

1Focus and method · What went well

The student explains why this method fits the question (a physical test of hundreds of uses is not realistic) and links the two kinds of evidence. The guide asks for rationale, not just a list of methods.

2Focus and method · What could be improved

Sources are selected for having numbers and a date, but no test of reliability is applied. Add selection criteria (author, peer review, independence, method stated) and say which sources you will trust most and why.

3Focus and method · What went well

Functional unit, system boundary and a stated equation make the method repeatable. A reader could rebuild the model.

4Presentation · Check against the current rules

The questionnaire itself is not shown. The guide allows an exemplar questionnaire in an appendix, but examiners do not read other appendix material. Keep all analysis in the main text.

3 Background: life-cycle thinking

≈609 words

3 Background: life-cycle thinking

3.1 Key ideas. A life-cycle assessment follows a product from raw materials to disposal. It reports impacts in categories, of which GWP100 is the best known. "Embodied energy" is the energy used to extract, process and manufacture a product before the user receives it. A stainless-steel bottle has high embodied energy because steel is made by melting and alloying metal at high temperature. In LCA terms a product comparison is only fair when the functional unit is the same for both options, and the system boundary decides which stages are counted.1

3.2 The single-use PET bottle. Summa et al. (2025), working in Italy, report 0.0925 kg CO2e to produce one 500 mL PET bottle. A US web page that summarises PET bottle studies gives ranges per 500 mL bottle of 0.0379–0.0625 kg CO2e for material production, 0.034–0.046 for fabrication and 0.0002 for bottling (NEMS, n.d.). Adding these three stages gives 0.072–0.109 kg CO2e, which brackets the figure from Summa et al. The same page lists a bottle mass of 13.3–26 g and a recycling credit of 0.0114 kg CO2e at disposal. The author of the page is not named.2

3.3 The stainless-steel bottle. Summa et al. (2025) report 1.0179 kg CO2e to produce one stainless-steel bottle, which is about eleven times the PET figure. Diaz-Loar (2024) takes a 500 mL bottle to contain 198 g of stainless steel and quotes World Steel figures of about 6.8 tonnes CO2 per tonne of stainless steel with 30% recycled content, and about 2.1 tonnes per tonne with 85% recycled content. Berical (2025) quotes 543 g CO2e per bottle from the World Stainless Association. Thornton and Lockrey (2018) report that steel and aluminium bottles had the highest material and production impacts of the bottles compared, because of the intensity of the materials and manufacturing and the extra weight.

3.4 What the sources say about break-even. The University of Wisconsin-Madison (2017) estimates 800 g CO2e for a 750 mL refillable bottle and 280 g for a 750 mL bottle of water, and concludes that the refillable bottle wins after about three refills. Berical (2025) derives 7 uses from the 543 g figure, but then adopts 482 uses from Diaz-Loar (2024), who assumes stainless steel breaks even like aluminium and states that no study measures this directly. KeepCup (2026) says non-insulated stainless bottles break even after 10–30 uses, and insulated bottles after 30–90 uses. Olatayo et al. (2021) studied plastic bottles in Johannesburg and found that a reusable bottle had lower impact than single-use bottles in all 13 categories they assessed, with global warming of 0.1462 kg CO2e for the reusable bottle against 0.5128 kg for single-use over their one-year functional unit.34

The sources therefore disagree widely, from about three uses to nearly five hundred. Some of the difference comes from what each source measures. The University of Wisconsin-Madison figure includes the water in the bottled product, the Johannesburg study uses a one-year functional unit, and the Diaz-Loar figure is an assumption rather than a measurement. This is why I build my own model in section 4 from the best-documented production figures.

3.5 The school context. At my school, students can refill bottles at water coolers on each floor, and the canteen sells bottled water. This means a student can choose either option on any school day, so the break-even comparison describes a real daily choice. The cooler water is the same for both options, which supports excluding the water from the system boundary.

1Knowledge and understanding · What went well

Functional unit, system boundary, GWP100 and embodied energy are explained in the student's own words and then used later in the essay. Terminology is accurate and not overdone.

2Knowledge and understanding · What could be improved

Numbers are quoted accurately, but the NEMS page has no named author or date. Say plainly that its reliability is limited, or find the original studies it summarises.

3Knowledge and understanding · What could be improved

Section 3.4 reports each source in turn. It would be stronger to group them: which sources measured, which assumed, which come from companies. That would show understanding of why they disagree.

4Knowledge and understanding · Check against the current rules

The guide says literature should come mostly from acknowledged design, technology and scientific sources. Here most of the sources are websites or company pages. Replace some with peer-reviewed or institutional reports.

4 Analysis: a break-even model

≈459 words

4 Analysis: a break-even model

4.1 Inputs. Table 1 lists the inputs. For production footprints I use the figures from Summa et al. (2025), because they come from one peer-reviewed study that measured both bottles with the same method. For washing, I assume the bottle is refilled twice a school day (the most common answer in my survey) and washed once a week in an energy-class A dishwasher. Summa et al. (2025) give 0.32 kg CO2e per wash cycle for this class of machine. I assume a full load of 20 items, so one bottle carries 0.32 / 20 = 0.016 kg CO2e per wash. With 10 uses per week this gives w = 0.0016 kg CO2e per use. These are my assumptions, not figures from the paper.1

4.2 Worked example (base case). n = Pr / (p − w) = 1.0179 / (0.0925 − 0.0016) = 1.0179 / 0.0909 = 11.2. The reusable bottle has a lower cumulative footprint from the 12th use onward. At two refills a day this is reached in about six school days.2

4.3 Sensitivity. Table 2 changes one input at a time. A higher production footprint for the steel bottle, calculated from the World Steel figures quoted by Diaz-Loar (0.198 kg × 6.8 = 1.35 kg), moves the break-even to 15 uses. A low value using the 85% recycled figure (0.198 × 2.1 = 0.42 kg) gives 5 uses. Using the lowest and highest PET production values from NEMS (0.072 and 0.109) gives 15 and 10 uses. Washing the bottle daily instead of weekly changes the result only from 12 to 13 uses.3

4.4 Washing is the weak point. The last row shows that if a bottle were washed alone in a machine every day, it would never break even, because 0.16 kg per wash is larger than the 0.0925 kg saved by not buying a PET bottle. Summa et al. (2025) found that the washing method was the largest part of the yearly impact of reusable bottles, so w is my least certain input. A full dishwasher load or a quick hand rinse keeps w small.4

4.5 Comparison with the literature. My break-even values of 5–15 uses are close to the 7 uses derived by Berical (2025) and the 3 refills of the University of Wisconsin-Madison (2017), and below the 10–30 uses of KeepCup (2026). They are far below the 482 uses assumed by Diaz-Loar (2024). I think the 482 figure is less reliable for stainless steel because it was assumed from aluminium, and Summa et al. measured the two bottle types directly. Olatayo et al. (2021) support the direction of the result, as a reusable plastic bottle lowered global warming impact in Johannesburg, although their reusable bottle is plastic and their assumptions differ.5

1Critical thinking · What went well

The inputs are in a table with sources, and the student states which values are their own assumptions. This honesty lets the reader judge the model.

2Critical thinking · What went well

A worked calculation is shown once and the rest is tabulated. This is the approach the guide recommends for calculated results.

3Critical thinking · What could be improved

Only one input is changed at a time, and no ranges are combined. Try a best case and a worst case together, and explain what an uncertainty of, say, ±20% on production would do to the answer.

4Critical thinking · What could be improved

Good insight that washing matters, but the extreme "alone every day" case is not realistic and is not linked back to the survey. Say how many students actually wash like this.

5Critical thinking · What could be improved

The gap between 5–15 uses and 482 uses is noticed but not explained. The reason given ("assumed from aluminium") is the source's own wording; show what an aluminium-based figure would imply for stainless steel, or say that you cannot resolve it.

5 Primary data: survey and product analysis

≈427 words

5 Primary data: survey and product analysis

5.1 Survey results. Of the 40 students, 31 (78%) owned a reusable bottle, and 12 (30%) said they bought bottled water at school on three or more days a week. Among the 31 owners, 14 (45%) had a stainless-steel bottle, 15 (48%) a plastic one, and 2 (6%) another material. The most common number of refills per school day was two (14 students, 45%). Eleven owners (35%) washed their bottle daily, 12 (39%) weekly and 8 (26%) rarely or never. Nine owners (29%) had lost or replaced a bottle in the last 12 months, and two (6%) said they lost a bottle within two weeks of buying it.1

5.2 Use in the model. Refilling twice a day supports the 10 uses per week in the base case. Weekly or daily washing is the commonest behaviour and is covered by the scenarios in Table 2. The 26% who rarely wash the bottle matter for hygiene, though my footprint model gives them a lower washing impact. The two students who lost a bottle within two weeks (at two refills a day, about 20 uses) may have lost it before or soon after the break-even point of 12–15 uses, so for them the stainless bottle may barely have repaid its footprint.2

5.3 Product analysis. Table 3 shows the three bottles. Bottle A is a double-wall insulated stainless-steel bottle, Bottle B a single-wall stainless bottle, and Bottle C a reusable plastic bottle. To estimate the footprint of A and B, I multiplied their measured mass by the 6.8 kg CO2e per kg (30% recycled) figure from Diaz-Loar (2024). This gives 0.312 × 6.8 = 2.12 kg for A and 0.168 × 6.8 = 1.14 kg for B. Using the model in section 4 with these values gives a break-even of 24 uses for A and 13 uses for B. Bottle C is not modelled because I did not find a production figure for a plastic reusable bottle.3

All 40 forms were returned, and each question was multiple choice so that answers could be counted. The product analysis used one measurement per bottle, which is acceptable for masses of 95–312 g on a scale that reads to 1 g, but it gives no estimate of spread.

The insulated bottle keeps water cold, which may encourage refilling in hot weather, but I did not test this. The lighter single-wall bottle pays back its footprint sooner, so the design that is best for sustainability may not be the one students prefer to carry.4

1Critical thinking · What could be improved

The results are only percentages of one sample of 40. Cross-tabulate (for example, do stainless-steel owners wash more often than plastic owners?) and say how confident you can be with 31 owners.

2Critical thinking · What went well

The survey is linked straight back to the model inputs (refills per day, washing, loss). Primary data are used to test assumptions rather than just to decorate the essay.

3Critical thinking · What could be improved

The estimate for Bottles A and B multiplies mass by a per-kg factor from a different source, and assumes Summa et al. used a bottle of similar mass. This assumption is not tested or stated as a limitation.

4Critical thinking · What went well

The finding that the double-wall bottle needs about twice the uses is original and well suited to a DT essay, because it connects a design feature to footprint.

5Presentation · Check against the current rules

Figures 2 and 3 show only simple counts that are already in the text. The guide warns that too many graphs distract; keep one chart and put the rest in a table.

6 Evaluation

≈352 words

6 Evaluation

6.1 Reliability of sources. The two LCA papers are peer reviewed, but Summa et al. (2025) use an Italian electricity mix and Olatayo et al. (2021) study Johannesburg, so neither describes Dubai. The NEMS page has no named author. KeepCup (2026) sells reusable cups, and its body text (10–30 uses) and summary table (20–40 uses) give different ranges for the same bottle type. Berical (2025) repeats a figure from the World Stainless Association, which represents the stainless-steel industry. The Diaz-Loar paper is a working paper and has not been through the same peer review.1

6.2 Limitations of the model. I counted only GWP100 and excluded transport, disposal and the water itself, so the model says nothing about water use, toxicity or littering. I used a typical PET bottle rather than an rPET bottle. Recycled content would lower the footprint of the single-use bottle and push the break-even to more uses, so my result may be a little optimistic for the reusable bottle. The washing figures use my own assumption of a full dishwasher load. The survey had 40 students from two classes in one school and may not represent the whole school.2

6.3 Reliability of the primary data. The survey used a convenience sample and relied on self-reported behaviour. Students may overstate how often they wash their bottle, and two Grade 11 classes cannot represent younger students, who may lose bottles more often. The bottle masses were measured once each, so I cannot give an uncertainty. I also did not reach a prototype or test stage of the design cycle, which the guide treats as optional but which would have given first-hand performance data on, for example, how long insulated bottles keep water cool.

6.4 Effect on the conclusion. These limitations would change the exact break-even number, but I do not think they change the main finding, because even the highest value I found with measured data (24 uses) is a few weeks of use. Further work could use LCA software with UAE electricity data and test how long students really keep their bottles.34

1Critical thinking · What went well

The student comments on the reliability of each source and spots an internal inconsistency in the KeepCup page. This is the kind of critical reading the guide asks for.

2Critical thinking · What could be improved

Limitations are listed but their effect is not measured. For rPET, run the model with a lower p to show how much the break-even moves instead of saying it "may be a little optimistic".

3Critical thinking · What could be improved

"I do not think they change the main finding" is asserted, not shown. A critical evaluation tests the claim with the model, for example with the worst combination of inputs.

4Critical thinking · Check against the current rules

The guide asks for critical comment on validity and reliability, and for unresolved issues to be named with suggestions for further work. The last sentence starts this but is very brief.

7 Conclusion

≈341 words

7 Conclusion

The research question asked to what extent a reusable stainless-steel bottle is a more sustainable choice than a single-use rPET bottle for students in Dubai. The evidence suggests that it is the better choice to a large extent, provided it is used enough times and washed sensibly.

As shown in section 4, a stainless-steel bottle costs about 1.02 kg CO2e to make, compared with about 0.09 kg for a PET bottle, so it must be used about 12 times to break even in the base case. Across the scenarios the range is 5 to 15 uses, and 24 uses for the heavier double-wall bottle. A student who refills twice a day reaches this in one to two school weeks. The survey showed that most students who own a reusable bottle refill it at least twice a day, so the benefit is likely to be reached. The results agree with several sources, such as the three refills in the University of Wisconsin-Madison (2017) estimate, but not with the 482 uses assumed by Diaz-Loar (2024).1

Two conditions decide whether the reusable bottle is really more sustainable. First, it must not be lost early: two of the 31 owners lost their bottle within two weeks. Second, it must not be washed in a way that adds more emissions than the bottle saves, as in the worst case in Table 2. Based on this, I would suggest that the school encourages lighter single-wall bottles, runs bottles through full dishwasher loads, and puts name labels on bottles to reduce loss. The school could also add more water refill points and raise awareness among students, which would make a real green difference.23

In short, the evidence points in one direction, and the main message for a student is simple: choose a reusable bottle, keep it, and wash it in a full load.

The essay answers only the global warming part of "sustainable". Water use, toxicity, rPET data and UAE-specific electricity remain open questions that a longer study could address.4

1Critical thinking · What went well

The conclusion answers the question directly ("to a large extent") and names the two conditions that limit the answer.

2Critical thinking · What could be improved

The second paragraph mostly repeats numbers from sections 4 and 5. A conclusion should synthesise (what do the results mean together?) rather than restate them.

3Focus and method · What could be improved

The recommendations to the school (single-wall bottles, labels, refill points) go beyond the evidence collected. Label them as ideas for further testing, or support them with data.

4Knowledge and understanding · What could be improved

"A real green difference" is loose language again. Use the measured quantity (for example, "lower GWP100").
