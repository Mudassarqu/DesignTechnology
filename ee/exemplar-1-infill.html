1 Introduction

≈492 words

1.1 Context

School makerspaces have made functional 3D printing routine. In my school’s design workshop, students and staff print jigs, hooks, cable guides and shelf brackets for classroom use. A part can be made in a few hours, but every part uses plastic filament, and over-built or failed parts become waste. Kirch and Saloni (2026) name failed prints, support structures and design errors as sources of PLA waste in university makerspaces and state that most of this material currently ends up in landfill, although they give no quantities. Kellens et al. (2017) also point to design for less material as one way of reducing the environmental burden of additive manufacturing.1

1.2 The design problem

In an interview, the workshop technician told me that load-bearing parts such as shelf brackets are normally printed at 50% grid infill “to be safe”. There is no testing behind that choice. A slicer offers many infill patterns and densities (Prusa Research, n.d.), so it is possible that the same load could be carried with less plastic. The design problem is therefore to find the lowest-mass print profile that still meets a known load requirement, and to base that choice on evidence from the workshop’s own printer instead of habit.2

1.3 Research question

To what extent do infill pattern and infill density affect the strength-to-mass ratio of FDM-printed PETG shelf brackets, and which setting lets a school workshop use the least filament while still carrying the load of a classroom shelf?3

1.4 Scope and key terms

Infill is the internal structure that a slicer prints inside the outer walls of a part. Infill density is the share of the interior volume that is filled with plastic. Failure load is the load at which a bracket either fractures or has dropped by 10 mm at its tip, whichever happens first. Strength-to-mass ratio is the failure load in newtons divided by the mass of the bracket in grams. I use this ratio because the aim is to save filament, so a bracket that carries the same load for less mass is better.4

I keep one bracket geometry, one material (PETG), one printer and three patterns (grid, triangles, gyroid) at three densities (15%, 30% and 50%), with a solid bracket as a reference. This keeps the investigation small enough for about 40 hours of work. Other materials, other printers and long-term loading are outside the scope, but I discuss what they might change in section 5.

1.5 Approach and independence

I follow the design cycle: investigate the need and the literature, design a bracket and a test rig, make the prints, test them, evaluate the evidence and then recommend a school print profile as a short design specification. This essay is independent of my design project. The bracket, the rig, the literature and every data point used here were produced for this essay, and no material, model or data from the design project appears in it.5

2 Method and design of the investigation

≈667 words

2.1 Why a bracket-level experiment

The question asks about the performance of a printed product, so I decided to test printed brackets, not test pieces. Vraitch, Prince and Souppez (2023) tested standard ISO 178 coupons in three-point bending, including PETG, but a coupon does not show how a real L-shaped part fails at its corner. Published data therefore guided my expectations, while my own tests answer the question for my geometry and my printer. Secondary research explained the results and gave me a sense check. I used peer-reviewed or manufacturer sources for numbers (Ahn et al., 2002; Vraitch et al., 2023; Kellens et al., 2017; Prusa Polymers, 2023) and two general web sources only to describe infill patterns (Prusa Research, n.d.; Boissonneault, 2022). The technician interview gave the real use case.1

2.2 Design specification for the bracket

The technician described a typical classroom shelf: 600 mm wide, carrying about 12 kg of textbooks on two brackets. Each bracket must therefore carry about 6 kg, which is 6 × 9.81 = 59 N. I applied a factor of safety of 2.5, so the target failure load is 147 N. This factor is my judgement. It is larger than I would need for a tested metal part because print quality varies from part to part, I could not control filament moisture, and I could not test long-term creep. The bracket is an L-profile modelled in CAD: arm 100 mm, leg 80 mm, width 25 mm, thickness 12 mm, a 5 mm inside fillet, two 4.5 mm screw holes in the leg and a hook slot 95 mm from the wall face.2

2.3 Variables and print settings

Table 1 shows what I changed, measured and controlled. I print one L-profile lying flat so that the 25 mm width is built from layers and the bending acts along the printed roads. This follows Ahn et al. (2002), who found that load direction relative to the roads strongly affects strength, so I fixed it to avoid a hidden second variable.3

2.4 The test rig

Figure 1 shows the rig. A plywood plate is clamped to the bench and the bracket is fixed to it with two M4 screws and washers. A bucket hangs from the hook slot at a = 95 mm. I treat the arm as a cantilever. A fixed ruler marks a 10 mm drop, and a catch box with foam sits underneath because the sand and bucket weigh over 20 kg.4

2.5 Procedure

  1. Print three brackets for each of the nine pattern and density combinations, plus three solid brackets, in an order set by drawing numbers from a bag. Mark each with a code.
  2. After 24 hours, weigh each bracket on the 0.1 g scale and record the mass.
  3. Screw a bracket to the plate and check the hook slot is 95 mm from the wall face.
  4. Pour sand into the bucket at about 100 g/s, which raises the load at about 1 N/s.
  5. Stop at fracture or when the tip has dropped 10 mm on the ruler. Weigh the bucket and sand on the bathroom scale and calculate F = mass × 9.81.
  6. Record the failure mode and photograph the break. Repeat for all 30 brackets.

2.6 Uncertainty and data processing

The bathroom scale reads in 0.1 kg steps, and sand keeps falling for a moment after failure, so I estimate the load reading at ±0.4 kg, which is ±4 N. For each condition I calculate the mean of the three loads and their half-range. I add the 4 N to the half-range to give a cautious combined uncertainty. Relative uncertainties in load and mass are added to give the uncertainty of the ratio. With only three repeats, the half-range is a rough guide, not a statistical confidence interval, and I do not claim statistical significance.5

Safety: eye protection, a catch box, nobody standing in line with the arm, and a risk assessment approved by the technician.

3 Background knowledge

≈699 words

3.1 Fused deposition modelling and anisotropy

In fused deposition modelling (FDM) a heated nozzle extrudes a thermoplastic into thin roads that are laid in layers and bond to each other as they cool. Because the bond between roads is weaker than the road itself, an FDM part is anisotropic: its strength depends on the direction of the load. Ahn et al. (2002) tested ABS and found that raster orientation and air gap had the greatest effect on tensile strength. Axial specimens, where the roads lay along the load, were strongest, and transverse specimens, where only the bonds between roads carried the load, were weakest. The same paper reports that FDM specimens reached roughly 10 to 73 percent of the strength of injection-moulded ABS at zero air gap, depending on orientation. This is the reason that I fixed orientation and why my results apply only to one print direction.1

3.2 PETG

PETG is a glycol-modified polyethylene terephthalate that is widely used for functional printed parts. The manufacturer’s data sheet for the filament I used gives a density of 1.27 g/cm³, a flexural strength of 60 ± 1 MPa for horizontally printed specimens and 64 ± 1 MPa for vertical ones (both ISO 178), and heat deflection temperatures of 74 °C at 0.45 MPa and 68 °C at 1.80 MPa (Prusa Polymers, 2023). I treat these as an upper reference for a well-printed solid part, not as a prediction for my brackets, because the data sheet is written by the supplier and the specimens were not my geometry. The heat deflection temperatures show that the bracket will be safe in an air-conditioned classroom but should not be used near heat sources.2

3.3 Infill patterns and how they carry load

Slicers fill the interior with a repeating pattern. Grid infill crosses two sets of straight lines, and each layer repeats the same two-dimensional pattern. Triangles infill uses three line directions. From structural mechanics, a triangle is the one polygon that cannot change shape without a member changing length, so triangulated lines can resist shear in the layer plane. This reasoning is my own and I treat it as a hypothesis to test. A gyroid is a triply periodic minimal surface, printed as continuous wavy lines that differ from layer to layer. Boissonneault (2022) describes it as nearly isotropic, although not technically isotropic, because of its cubic symmetry, and notes that high densities with closely spaced waves can cause printer vibration.3

Vraitch et al. (2023) printed ABS, PLA, PLA+ and PETG in three-point bending. For PLA they compared lines, gyroid and triangles from 10% to 100%, and for PETG they tested gyroid at five densities. They report that modulus and strength are independent of the tested infill types, with an exception for PLA triangles at 60% and above, and that mechanical properties increase linearly with infill density. I use this as a hypothesis: load capacity should rise about linearly with density, and pattern should matter less than density.4

3.4 Bending of a cantilever

The flexural stress in a three-point bend test is given as σ = 3FL / 2wd² (Design World, n.d.). My rig is a cantilever with load F at distance a. The bending moment at the root is M = Fa. For a rectangular section of width b and depth d, the second moment of area is I = bd³/12 and the extreme fibre is d/2 from the neutral axis, so σ = M(d/2)/I = 6Fa / bd². This assumes a solid, uniform section, so for infilled brackets it is only a guide to the order of magnitude. Stress is also higher at the fillet than the formula predicts.5

3.5 Material use and sustainability

Kellens et al. (2017) review the environmental dimensions of additive manufacturing and report that its specific energy use is one to two orders of magnitude higher than for conventional processes, largely because of slow process rates. They also show that redesign for less material can reduce part volume substantially in some applications. Their examples are mostly industrial, so for a school printer I use them only to support the principle that using less material per part reduces burden, not to estimate the size of the benefit.6

4.2 Effect of density

Density has the clearest effect. For every pattern the mean failure load rises by about 65 to 75 N between 15% and 50% infill, which is much larger than the combined uncertainty of 10 to 13 N on each point. For gyroid the load rises from 138 N to 203 N. A straight line through the gyroid means gives a slope of (203 − 138) / 0.35 ≈ 186 N per unit density and an intercept of 110 N, which I interpret as the contribution of the perimeters and skins. Extrapolating this line to 100% predicts 296 N, close to the 300 N measured for the solid reference. The grid line predicts 289 N and the triangles line 319 N. This agrees with Vraitch et al. (2023), who report a linear increase of properties with density. It also shows that about 35% of the solid bracket’s capacity comes from the walls alone, which matters for the recommendation.2

4.3 Effect of pattern

Pattern has a smaller effect. At 15% the three patterns lie within 10 N of each other (131 to 141 N), which is inside the uncertainty. At 50%, triangles reached 214 N and grid 196 N, a difference of 18 N against an uncertainty of about 12 N on each, so the evidence for a real difference is weak. Gyroid sits between the two. Figure 3 plots load against mass. The three pattern lines are close, with triangles and gyroid slightly above grid. This is consistent with Vraitch et al. (2023), who found little influence of pattern, and I cannot confirm my own hypothesis that triangulation helps.3

4.4 Strength-to-mass ratio

The ratio is almost constant. Grid values are 4.46, 4.55 and 4.50 N/g, triangles 4.72, 4.78 and 4.78 N/g, and gyroid 4.74, 4.86 and 4.75 N/g. The solid bracket gives 4.80 N/g. Every ratio has an uncertainty of about ±0.2 to ±0.4 N/g, so the nine infilled brackets cannot be separated from each other or from the solid bracket. In other words, removing infill removes strength in proportion to the mass that is removed. A lower density does not give a “free” saving of load per gram, and a bracket at 15% is not stronger per gram than a solid one. The question “to what extent” therefore has two answers: infill density changes the load capacity a great deal, but changes the strength-to-mass ratio very little.4

4.5 Failure modes and a sense check

At 15% infill, eight of nine brackets fractured at the inside fillet before the tip had dropped 10 mm. At 50%, seven of nine reached the 10 mm drop first, with visible whitening of the plastic at the arm root and no fracture. The solid brackets fractured at the fillet in two of three tests. The failure at the fillet agrees with section 3.4, which predicts higher stress there than the simple formula gives.

As a sense check, I put the data-sheet flexural strength of 60 MPa into σ = 6Fa / bd² with a = 0.095 m, b = 0.025 m and d = 0.012 m. This gives F = σbd² / 6a = (60 × 10⁶ × 0.025 × 0.012²) / (6 × 0.095) ≈ 380 N. My solid brackets failed at 300 N, about 79% of this value. A shortfall is expected because of the stress concentration at the fillet and because printed bonds between roads are weaker than the data-sheet specimens, so the result is plausible. It does not prove the data are correct.5

4.6 Meeting the design requirement

The target is 147 N. None of the 15% brackets reached it on average (131, 141 and 138 N). Every 30% profile exceeded it: grid 160 N, triangles 172 N and gyroid 168 N. The lowest individual 30% result was 151 N (grid), 165 N (triangles) and 160 N (gyroid), so each passed even at its weakest print, though by a small margin for grid. Of the three 30% profiles, gyroid has the lowest mass at 34.6 g and a minimum of 160 N, 9% above the target. Compared with the technician’s 50% grid bracket (43.6 g), 30% gyroid saves 9.0 g, or 21%, per bracket.6

5 Discussion and evaluation

≈696 words

5.1 Reliability and validity of the data

Three repeats per condition are the biggest weakness. With n = 3 the half-range is a rough indicator, and one weak print can move the mean by several newtons. The data come from one printer, one spool of one PETG and one bracket geometry. PETG absorbs moisture, and the filament was stored in the workshop without a dry box. Humidity was not recorded, and I cannot rule out that moisture changed layer bonding between the first and last prints. I randomised the print order so that any drift would be spread across conditions, which reduces systematic bias but not scatter. I judged the rig itself to be valid for the question because it loads the real part in the way the part is used, but it has limits. Sand was poured by hand, so the loading rate varied by perhaps 20%, and I did not measure rig compliance. The 10 mm criterion is a judgement and depends on where I stopped pouring.1

5.2 What the tests did not cover

Shelves carry a load for months, so creep is the most important gap. The data are short-term failure loads taken in a few minutes, and PETG, like other thermoplastics, may deform slowly under a sustained load. This is why I used a factor of safety of 2.5 and why I cannot call the recommended bracket safe for permanent use. I tested only one print orientation. Ahn et al. (2002) show that orientation can change strength greatly, and a bracket printed standing up would load the weak bonds between layers. The data sheet gives a smaller difference between horizontal and vertical printed specimens (39 and 42 MPa tensile, 60 and 64 MPa flexural), but those are other specimens and tests, so I cannot transfer them to my brackets.2

5.3 Comparison with the literature

My results agree with Vraitch et al. (2023) on two points: load increases about linearly with density, and pattern matters less than density. The agreement is encouraging because their specimens, geometry and loading were different from mine. I did not test above 50%, so I cannot comment on their finding for PLA triangles at 60% and above. I also cannot say whether the gyroid advantage in handling reported by Boissonneault (2022) would appear in larger parts. Several of the sources are limited. The data sheet is supplier data, the Boissonneault and Prusa pages are general guidance, and Kirch and Saloni (2026) report the existence of waste but not its quantity. I treated them as context and not as evidence for numbers.3

5.4 How much filament would be saved?

The technician estimated that the workshop prints about 100 load-bearing parts a year. If each saved 9 g, the saving would be about 0.9 kg a year, around one spool of filament. This is real but small. Kirch and Saloni (2026) identify failed prints and supports as makerspace waste sources, and the workshop’s print log was not available to me, so I cannot say how a 0.9 kg saving compares with the waste from failed prints. A reasonable view is that choosing the right infill is a low-cost habit change that is worth making, but not the largest lever. Reducing failed prints and supports might save more, and this is a topic for further investigation.4

5.5 Recommended school print profile

As my design specification, I recommend PETG, 0.4 mm nozzle, 235 °C, 0.2 mm layers, 2 perimeters, 5 top and bottom layers and 30% gyroid infill, printed flat as in this test, for brackets that carry up to 6 kg each on a dry indoor shelf. The workshop should label this as a tested profile, check each batch with one proof test at 147 N, and not use it for people-carrying, overhead or safety-critical parts. If the shelf must carry more, the technician should move to 50% triangles or gyroid, not return to a default of 50% grid for every part. The margin of 9% above the target at the weakest 30% gyroid bracket is thin. Given the untested creep and humidity, I would ask the workshop to run a 4-week sustained-load trial before wider adoption.5

6 Conclusion

≈227 words

In answer to the research question, infill density affects the load capacity of FDM-printed PETG shelf brackets to a large extent, but it affects the strength-to-mass ratio very little. Across 30 brackets, load rose by roughly 185 to 210 N per unit of density, and the ratio stayed within about 4.5 to 4.9 N/g for all nine infilled profiles and the solid reference, with an uncertainty of roughly ±0.4 N/g. Infill pattern had a smaller effect than density. Triangles and gyroid reached slightly higher loads than grid, but the differences were mostly inside the uncertainty, so I cannot claim that one pattern is better.1

For the school workshop, the evidence supports the second part of the question. The lowest density that met the 147 N target for a 6 kg shelf load was 30%, and the 30% gyroid profile had the lowest mass of the three, 34.6 g. It saved 9.0 g, or 21%, of filament per bracket compared with the current 50% grid practice, which would be about 0.9 kg a year at the technician’s estimate of use. This recommendation is conditional on dry filament, flat printing, short-term loading and a proof test. Limits that remain are small n, one printer, uncontrolled humidity and untested creep. A longer sustained-load trial, a second orientation and a second printer would be the next steps.2

1Critical thinking · What went well

The conclusion answers both parts of the RQ directly and uses the evidence (numbers, uncertainty), instead of repeating the methods. It also states what was not shown, so it is consistent with the analysis.

2Critical thinking · What went well

First reflection · September. I began with the idea of testing brackets after seeing the technician print every load-bearing part at 50% grid “to be safe”. My first research question was far too wide (“Is 3D printing sustainable?”). My supervisor asked me what I could actually measure in a workshop. That changed my approach: I stopped reading general articles on sustainability and started sketching a rig, and I noticed that I could not write the RQ until I could name the measurement. I decided to fix the material and the geometry so that pattern and density were the only things that changed. My main concern is that I have no experience of experimental uncertainty, so I booked a session with the physics technician.1

Interim reflection · December. The first test rig failed: the plywood plate flexed, so my first three results were measuring the rig as much as the bracket. I stiffened the plate with a second sheet and clamped it at two points, and I discarded the earlier data and said why in my log. This taught me that a result is only useful if I can explain what produced it. Reading Vraitch et al. changed my mind about the triangles pattern: I had expected a large benefit, and their conclusion that pattern matters less than density made me treat my own idea as a hypothesis instead of a result. I also realised that “strength-to-mass” was a better measure than “strength”, because my aim is to save plastic. I am worried about humidity in PETG and I now record it as a limitation.2

Final reflection (viva voce) · February. The data showed that the ratio was almost constant, which was not what I wanted to find. At first I tried to find a pattern that “won”, then my supervisor asked what I would conclude if the uncertainty were real. I rewrote the analysis around the uncertainty and the essay became more honest and more useful. I developed skills in uncertainty analysis, careful writing of a method and judging sources. If I did the research again I would run at least five prints per condition, add a dry box and start the creep trial in November so that it could be in the essay. I now ask “what would change my mind?” before I trust a result.3
