1 Introduction

≈403 words

1 Introduction

Carrying a school bag is a daily task for almost every secondary student. Brackley and Stevenson (2004) reviewed epidemiological, physiological and biomechanical research and concluded that a limit of 10–15% of body mass is a justified weight limit for children's backpacks. They also called for more research on how load, bag design and personal factors interact. This matters for a designer: the load is set by the student's timetable, but the bag is a product, and a product can be redesigned. The part of the bag that meets the body is the shoulder strap, so this essay investigates one design feature that sits directly under the load.

My interest began on the stairs. In a short needs survey of 40 Grade 11 students (Table 1), most reported shoulder ache after carrying their bag upstairs, and about two thirds had at least once swapped to one strap because both felt uncomfortable.1 Many bags in my year group have narrow or thin straps. A narrow strap should concentrate the same force on a smaller area, but a designer cannot simply keep widening it: a wider strap uses more material, can rub the neck and may be harder to adjust. The design question is therefore one of trade-off, not just "wider is better".

The research question is: To what extent does shoulder-strap width affect perceived discomfort for Grade 11 students carrying a 10% body-mass school-bag load, and what strap width should a redesigned strap specify?2

The question has two parts. "To what extent" asks for a measured size of effect, and the second part makes the essay end in a design decision, as a design technology essay should. I focus on Grade 11 students because they are the users of the redesigned strap and because the 10% load is within the range supported by Brackley and Stevenson (2004). The essay does not investigate the safe load itself, hip belts, load placement or posture. Holding these fixed keeps the investigation to a single independent variable that I can manipulate and test.3

The essay follows the design cycle. Section 2 explains how I investigated user needs and tested three prototype straps. Section 3 gives the background and turns the needs into a specification. Section 4 presents and analyses the data. Section 5 evaluates the method and the data, and Section 6 gives a recommended strap width and answers the research question.4

2 Methodology

≈671 words

2 Methodology

Choice of method. I considered three ways of comparing strap widths. A manikin with pressure sensors, as used by Golriz et al. (2017), gives objective interface pressure but needs equipment my school does not have. Secondary data alone could not answer a question about my own users. I therefore chose a repeated-measures trial with human volunteers, because the research question is about perceived discomfort, which only a person can report, and added a calculated pressure estimate to give an objective comparison.1 A short interview after the trials captured comments the numbers could not.

Prototype straps. I made three pairs of straps (Figure 1) from polyester webbing over a 10 mm foam core, with widths of 25, 40 and 55 mm. Webbing in these widths was available from a local supplier. Quick-release buckles let me swap strap pairs on one 30-litre bag frame in under a minute, so every participant used the same bag. Strap length was adjusted to each participant but the foam, bag frame and stitching pattern were identical.

Participants, load and route. Sixteen Grade 11 volunteers (ten male, six female; body mass 48–81 kg, mean 62.4 kg) took part. Each carried 10% of their own body mass, made up of sealed bags of rice packed in the centre of the bag, because Golriz et al. (2017) found that load placement interacts with strap width, so I kept the placement constant. The route was a 600 m loop that included one flight of stairs up and down, walked at a self-selected pace in 8–10 minutes. Every participant was timed on every trial to check that pace did not differ much between straps.2

Order and rest. Participants completed all three strap conditions on the same day with 15 minutes' seated rest between trials. There are six possible orders of three straps. I allocated orders 1–4 to three participants each and orders 5–6 to two each, so no order was used more than three times.3

Pilot. Before the main trials I tested the procedure with three volunteers who are not in the main sample. The pilot showed that the route was long enough for discomfort to build up, that swapping straps took about a minute, and that my first wording of the scale confused people because it asked about exertion. I rewrote the scale wording and moved the contact-length measurement to before the trial, when the strap was loaded but the participant was standing still, so that I could measure it accurately.

Rating scale. Immediately after each trial the participant rated discomfort in the shoulders on a 0–10 category-ratio scale based on the Borg CR10 (Borg, 1998), where 0 meant "nothing at all" and 10 meant "extremely strong". The CR10 is widely used in ergonomics for perceived exertion and discomfort (EHS.com, 2023). I adapted the wording from exertion to shoulder discomfort, so I treat the results as ratings on a CR10-style scale, not as validated Borg scores.

Pressure estimate. For each trial I estimated mean contact pressure as the force carried by one strap divided by its contact area. Contact length was measured with a flexible tape from where the strap first touched the shoulder to where it left it (mean of three readings), and contact width was taken as the strap width. Load was assumed to be shared equally by the two straps with no hip support.

Ethics and safety. The supervising teacher approved the plan. Participants gave written consent, could stop at any time, and were told the load would not exceed 10% of their mass. Anyone with current back or shoulder pain was excluded. Data were recorded against codes, not names.4

Independence from other work. This investigation uses a different product, materials, prototypes, literature and data from my design project (internal assessment). No model, test result or source is shared between the two.5

Sampling. Participants were volunteers from my own year group, so they are a convenience sample, not a random sample of Grade 11 students.6

3 Background and design specification

≈699 words

3 Background and design specification

Load, area and pressure. A shoulder strap transmits the weight of the bag to the body through the skin and soft tissue over the shoulder. The mean contact pressure is the force divided by the contact area, so for the same load a wider strap should reduce mean pressure. I use this physics only to support a design decision: pressure is the quantity a designer can change through strap geometry.1 Ergonomists also advise designing for the range of users, not the average user (Pheasant and Haslegrave, 2006), which is why I record body mass and contact length for each participant rather than relying on one figure.5

What published work suggests. Golriz et al. (2017) loaded a manikin with 20 kg and measured interface pressure at the shoulder with strap widths of 5, 6, 7 and 8 cm. Pressure fell significantly as width increased, and the effect of strap width interacted with whether the load was carried high or low in the bag. Their finding supports my hypothesis, but it came from a manikin at a heavier load and wider straps than mine (my widest strap is 5.5 cm), so it cannot tell me about perceived discomfort in people.2 Brackley and Stevenson (2004) support a 10–15% body-mass limit for children's backpacks, and I use the lower end, 10%, as a safe standard load for the trials.

Fischer (1987) measured pressure pain thresholds at nine muscle sites in 24 men and 26 women and found that sensitivity differs between muscles. This is a caution for my pressure estimate: a single "safe pressure" cannot be assumed to apply at the shoulder, so I compare straps with each other rather than with a threshold. Popular advice also favours wide straps. A health-system blog says that narrow straps can "dig into the shoulders" and recommends padded straps and a load of no more than 15% of body weight (UMass Memorial Health, 2026), but it gives no measured width or evidence.3

Why strap width, and not another feature? A designer has several ways to reduce shoulder discomfort: wider straps, thicker foam, curved (S-shaped) straps that follow the neck, a hip belt that moves load to the pelvis, a sternum strap, or a lighter bag. I rejected most of these for this essay for practical reasons. A hip belt changes how the load is shared, so my pressure calculation would no longer apply. Foam thickness would add a second variable, and a lighter bag is a question about the student's packing, not the product. Strap width is a single dimension I can change precisely with workshop materials, it is cheap to manufacture, and it can be compared directly with the widths of bags students already use (Table 3). Choosing one variable also keeps the design cycle manageable in the time available.

Who the user is. The users of the redesigned strap are Grade 11 students aged 16–17. They carry textbooks, a laptop and a water bottle, and use two flights of stairs several times a day, so a dynamic load is more realistic than a static one. I used a walk with stairs to reproduce this, because discomfort that appears only after a few minutes of movement would be missed by a test that just asked people to stand wearing the bag.

Existing bags. To see what students actually use, I measured the strap width of twelve bags brought by classmates (Table 3). Widths clustered at about 40–50 mm for standard school bags, with slimmer straps on fashion and laptop bags. My three test widths therefore span the range found in use.

Key terms. Interface pressure is the pressure between strap and body. A category-ratio scale is a numbered scale with verbal anchors that allows ratio-style comparisons of intensity. Counterbalancing varies the order of conditions so that fatigue or learning does not favour one condition. Validity is whether a measure captures what it claims to; reliability is whether it gives consistent results.4

Design specification. From the survey, the literature and the bag measurements I set the specification in Table 4. Each requirement is measurable so that the straps can be judged against it.

4 Results and analysis

≈549 words

4 Results and analysis

Table 5 gives the processed results; individual ratings are in the raw-data record kept with my research log.

1The mean rating falls as the strap gets wider, from 5.6 for the 25 mm strap to 3.9 for 40 mm and 3.3 for 55 mm (Figure 2). Three quarters of the participants rated the 25 mm strap at 5 or above, which is at the "hard" end of the scale, compared with a quarter for the 40 mm strap. The pattern is consistent across people: 13 of the 16 rated the 25 mm strap worst.

The size of each step needs care. Comparing the same person across straps, the mean drop from 25 mm to 40 mm was 1.7 points (SD of the differences 1.2), giving an approximate 95% confidence interval of 1.7 ± 0.6 points. The interval does not include zero, so the improvement is unlikely to be chance. From 40 mm to 55 mm the mean drop was only 0.6 points (SD 1.3), with an interval of 0.6 ± 0.7, which does include zero.2 Nine participants rated the 55 mm strap lowest, four rated the 40 mm strap lowest and three gave a tie, so the two wider straps cannot be separated on this evidence.5

Splitting the group by sex (ten males, six females) shows the same ordering of straps in both groups, with the six females giving slightly higher mean ratings (6.0, 4.3 and 3.5 for 25, 40 and 55 mm) than the ten males (5.4, 3.7 and 3.2). The groups are too small to say that this difference is real, but it is consistent with shorter contact lengths on narrower shoulders, which would concentrate the same force on less area. It is a reason to keep contact length as a recorded variable in any follow-up.

The pressure estimate shows why. Because pressure is inversely proportional to width, doubling the width halves the mean pressure, but each extra millimetre does less. Going from 25 to 40 mm cut the estimated pressure by 2.9 kPa (38%), while going from 40 to 55 mm cut it by only 1.3 kPa (27%). Dividing the change in rating by the change in pressure gives about 0.59 points per kPa for the first step and 0.46 points per kPa for the second, so the rating tracks pressure but flattens (Figure 3).3 With only three data points this is a pattern to be tested, not a fitted relationship.

The interviews add a reason for the flat result (Table 7). Fourteen of 16 participants said the 25 mm strap "dug in", but five said the 55 mm strap rubbed or pulled toward the neck, and three found it bulky. Comfort at 55 mm seems to be limited by a different problem from comfort at 25 mm.4 This matters for the specification, where neck rubbing is a separate requirement.

Compared with published work, the direction agrees with Golriz et al. (2017), who found lower pressure as width increased. My result adds that, for perceived discomfort in people at 10% load and widths of 25–55 mm, the benefit largely appears by 40 mm. Their widths (5–8 cm) and 20 kg load were different, so this is support for the direction and not a confirmation of the size.

5 Evaluation of method and data

≈598 words

5 Evaluation of method and data

Validity. The rating scale is an adaptation of the CR10 and was not validated for shoulder discomfort, so the numbers are best used to compare straps, not to say that 3.9 "is" a certain level of discomfort.1 The pressure estimate is a mean value. It assumes the strap lies flat, the contact length is the same for every strap and the load is shared equally, and it ignores peak pressure at strap edges and the extra force of walking on stairs. Fischer (1987) found that pressure sensitivity differs between muscles, so a calculated pressure is a useful comparison between straps, not proof of pain.

Order and carry-over effects. I counterbalanced the order, but orders 5 and 6 were used twice and orders 1–4 three times, so the design is not perfectly balanced. As a check, I averaged ratings by trial position. The mean was 4.0 for the first trial, 4.4 for the second and 4.4 for the third, suggesting a drift of about 0.4 points upward through the session, perhaps from fatigue. Because every strap appeared in every position, this drift should affect the straps similarly, but it adds uncertainty of a similar size to the 40 vs 55 mm difference.2

Reliability. Each participant used each strap once. I therefore cannot separate the effect of the strap from ordinary day-to-day variation within a person. Standard deviations of 1.3–1.5 points show wide differences between people, which is expected with different body shapes and contact lengths. A repeat of each condition on another day would show whether the same people give the same ratings.3

Bias. The straps were visibly different widths, so participants could form expectations. Many people believe wide straps feel better, and this may inflate the effect of width. I tried to reduce this by not telling participants which strap I expected to be best, but I did not hide the strap widths. In a repeat I would cover all straps in identical sleeves of the same colour.4

Sample. The sixteen participants were volunteers from my own year group and mostly my friends. They may be more willing to be careful or to please me, and ten males to six females does not reflect the school. The participants are also Grade 11 students; results should not be applied to younger students with smaller shoulders.

Generalising from three widths. Only three widths were tested, and they were chosen because they were available from one supplier, not because I knew they would bracket the best width. The data cannot locate an optimum between 40 and 55 mm, and the recommendation of 45 mm rests on reasoning about the neck, not on measurements. The relationship between width and discomfort may also differ for a longer journey: a ten-minute walk is realistic for moving between buildings, but not for the whole school day, when fatigue could magnify small differences.

Sources. The strongest sources are peer-reviewed: Brackley and Stevenson (2004), a review, and Golriz et al. (2017), a laboratory study. Fischer (1987) is old and clinical, so I use it only for caution. The EHS.com page and the health-system blog are secondary descriptions, and the blog has no named author or evidence, so I use it only to show what popular advice says.5

Unexpected outcomes. I expected a steady fall in discomfort as width increased. The flat result between 40 and 55 mm, with neck comments appearing at 55 mm, was not planned, but it changed the recommendation in Section 6, so I report it as part of the findings.

2Critical thinking · What could be improved

3Critical thinking · What could be improved

Without repeat trials the essay cannot estimate within-person variation. The paragraph explains this well, but the conclusion later does not carry the limit forward strongly enough.

4Critical thinking · What could be improved

6 Design recommendation and conclusion

6 Design recommendation and conclusion

Testing against the specification. Against Table 4, the 25 mm strap fails the comfort target (mean 5.6 and 75% rating 5 or above). The 40 mm strap meets it (mean 3.9; 25% rating 5 or above, exactly at the limit) and so does the 55 mm strap (mean 3.3; 19%). For neck contact, only the 55 mm strap fails the target, because five of 16 participants reported rubbing, compared with a limit of two. The 40 mm strap is the only tested width that meets both the comfort and neck requirements.1

Recommended specification. I recommend that the redesigned strap has a nominal width of 45 mm, a 10 mm foam core and a gently curved shape that moves the strap edge away from the neck. The evidence says that at least 40 mm is needed for comfort, and that 55 mm brings neck problems. A width of 45 mm sits between the two. It has not been tested, so it is a hypothesis for the next iteration of the design cycle: build a 45 mm strap with the curve, repeat the trial on a different day with hidden widths, and test it with a hip belt as a separate variable.2

Answer to the research question. Strap width has a large effect on perceived discomfort between 25 and 40 mm, where the rating fell by about 1.7 points on a 0–10 scale and the estimated pressure fell by 38%. Between 40 and 55 mm the effect is small and uncertain, and a new problem appears at the neck. A width of about 40–45 mm is the best-supported specification for Grade 11 students carrying 10% of body mass. Strap width is the dominant factor in shoulder discomfort for this group.34

Unresolved issues. The study cannot say whether the result holds for other loads, other foam thicknesses, longer walks, younger students or day-to-day variation. These are the next questions for further work.5

The recommendation is traced back to the numbered specification and to results, so the conclusion is built from the evidence, not repeated from earlier paragraphs.

2Critical thinking · What could be improved

45 mm is a sensible design inference but it is untested. The paragraph labels it as a hypothesis, which is honest. Keep that label in the conclusion and in the title of any summary.

3Critical thinking · What went well

4Critical thinking · What could be improved

"Strap width is the dominant factor" is stronger than the evidence. You varied only width, so you cannot say it matters more than load, padding or load position. Change to: "Width had a clear effect in this test."

Reflections on planning and progress form

293 words (limit 500)

First reflection session (September). I started with a broad idea, "a better school bag", and my supervisor asked what I could actually measure in 40 hours. I narrowed it to the shoulder strap because it is where the load meets the body and I could build prototypes cheaply. I had planned to use a pressure sensor mat, but the school does not have one, so I chose to combine ratings with a calculated pressure.1 My main concern was not mixing this with my design project, so I wrote a list of what I would not reuse.

Interim reflection (November). A pilot with three friends showed that my first scale wording confused them, because "exertion" is not the same as "discomfort", so I rewrote it and changed the order of trials after I noticed people got tired. I read Golriz et al. and found that load placement matters, which changed my plan: I fixed the position of the rice bags. I learned how much planning goes into a test before any data exists.2 I am behind on the background section.

Final reflection / viva (February). The data did not show what I expected between 40 mm and 55 mm. At first I thought I had made a mistake, but the interviews showed the neck problem. My supervisor pointed out that I could not claim an exact best width. If I did this again, I would hide the strap widths and repeat trials on another day. I now see a research question as something that the data can change.3 I developed skills in planning a controlled test, using a rating scale and writing evaluation, and I became more careful about the difference between what I measured and what I wanted to claim.4
