A

Title and research question

≈36 words

Design Technology EE

How can a gravity-assisted bag slide system integrated into the stairway railing at my school be designed to move student bags to the correct floor with minimal user effort and reliable stopping control?

1Focus and method · What could be improved

“How can … be designed” asks for a design, which makes this a design project, close to an IA. The EE must investigate a question and reach a reasoned conclusion. The guide also says an EE topic must not overlap with your design project.

2Focus and method · What could be improved

Three outcomes are packed into one question: floor selection, minimal effort and reliable stopping. Words such as “minimal” and “reliable” are not defined, so nobody can say what a full answer would look like.

3Focus and method · What went well

There is a real, interesting design problem here. The strongest idea in the whole draft (section 5) is that gravity moves the carrier but a separate mechanism must decide where it stops. That could be the whole essay.

4Focus and method · What could be improved

“at my school” and “my” are fine as context, but the question should work for any similar stairwell. Move the school detail to the introduction, and keep identifying names out of the essay.

1. Introduction

≈200 words

Students at school frequently carry bags between floors using the stairways. This can be inconvenient, particularly when bags are heavy or when students are carrying additional materials. A potential solution is a gravity-assisted bag transportation system integrated into the existing stairway railing. The system would allow a student to place a bag into a carrier, select the required floor, and allow gravity to move the carrier along a guided track.

However, designing such a system presents several technological challenges. The carrier must securely contain different types of school bags, the system must fit within the existing stairway environment, and the user should not need to apply significant physical effort. Most importantly, the carrier must stop reliably at the selected floor rather than continuing down the staircase.

This investigation therefore focuses on the design and development of a technological system, rather than investigating gravity as a physical phenomenon. Physics is used only where it supports decisions about the design.

The investigation considers: user requirements; ergonomic considerations; existing transportation and rail-guided systems; mechanism selection; materials; bag containment; floor-selection mechanisms; stopping and braking; safety; manufacturability; prototype performance.

The final objective is to establish a design specification for a feasible school bag transportation system.

1Focus and method · What went well

Clear, readable writing. You separate the design task from the physics, which tells the examiner what kind of essay this is.

2Focus and method · What could be improved

There is no evidence that the problem exists. How heavy are the bags, how far, how often? A measured fact (for example bag mass as a percentage of body mass) makes the problem real and gives you primary data.

3Focus and method · What could be improved

Eleven things to “consider” will not fit in 4,000 words, and each deserves evidence. Pick the one or two that the research question hangs on, probably stopping and safety, and treat the rest as constraints.

4Focus and method · What could be improved

“The final objective is to establish a design specification” describes a deliverable. State what you will find out, then how you will find it out. A specification is an outcome of research, not the answer to a question.

5Focus and method · Check against the current rules

The guide expects the essay to use the design cycle and to include both primary and secondary sources. Say here which parts of the cycle you will use and what primary data you will collect.

2. Identification of the design problem

≈148 words

The existing school environment requires students to transport bags manually between floors. A typical school bag can contain books, laptops, stationery and other equipment, increasing its weight.

The problem is not simply that the bag is heavy. The system must also consider:

  • User effort. The student should be able to load and operate the system without significant physical force.
  • Bag security. The bag should remain inside the carrier throughout transportation.
  • Floor selection. The user must be able to specify the destination floor.
  • Stopping reliability. The carrier must stop at the intended floor and remain stationary.
  • Safety. Moving components should not create accessible pinch or trapping points.
  • Compatibility with the school environment. The system should be capable of being integrated alongside an existing stairway railing without creating an obstruction.
  • Maintenance. Components that experience repeated contact or wear should be replaceable.

These requirements form the basis for the design specification.

1Focus and method · What went well

A well-organised list of requirements, with a one-line explanation of each. The framing “the problem is not simply that the bag is heavy” shows design thinking.

2Knowledge and understanding · What could be improved

These requirements are assumptions until a source or a result supports them. Add evidence for each one: survey data, a standard, an observation, or a measurement from the staircase.

3Focus and method · What could be improved

Gravity helps only when the carrier moves down. A student who needs the bag on a higher floor is not helped, and bags travel in both directions in the morning and the afternoon. Say whether the system is one-way, and how you will deal with the return trip. This affects whether the research question can be answered.

4Focus and method · Check against the current rules

Safety matters in any real stairwell. Moving objects near other students and the school’s fire and building rules are real constraints. Plan to test on a scale rig in the workshop, not on the school staircase, and check what your supervisor and the school require.

1Focus and method · What could be improved

This section is a plan (“could”, “should be conducted”), so the method is not yet described. For criterion A, say how many students, which year groups, how you chose them, how you worded the questions, and how you will protect their privacy.

2Critical thinking · What could be improved

The paragraph after the table states what “the results suggest” before any results exist. Never write findings before you have the data. When the data is in, write conclusions that match it, and say how far a small sample can speak for the school.

3Critical thinking · What could be improved

Percentages of agreement are a start but not analysis. Think about what you will compare, for example do students with heavier bags or higher floors want it more? What would change your design if the answer surprised you?

4Focus and method · What went well

The questions are relevant to the design: loading method, waiting time, control type and safety concerns each link to a possible design decision.

5Critical thinking · Check against the current rules

The guide expects numbers to be processed and presented as graphs or tables with analysis. A table of raw agreement counts will not earn analysis marks on its own.

1Focus and method · What could be improved

The text promises “measurable criteria”, but most targets are not measurable: “minimal force”, “acceptable position”, “common school bags”, “complicated procedures”. Give each a number and a unit, for example a maximum push force in newtons, a stopping tolerance in millimetres, a maximum carrier speed in metres per second, and a design load taken from your bag-mass data.

2Knowledge and understanding · What could be improved

Say where each number comes from: a survey result, a measurement, a standard or a published source. Targets that come from evidence are what make a specification credible.

3Focus and method · Check against the current rules

The guide asks for a method that someone else could repeat. A numeric specification also tells you how to test the prototype, which gives you the primary data section of the essay.

4Focus and method · What went well

The range is good, and “Stopping” and “Safety” are exactly the right things to track. Keep those two as the spine of the essay.

5. Existing product and system research

≈107 words

Existing technologies can provide design precedents. Examples include: conveyor systems; ski lifts; roller tracks; warehouse gravity conveyors; luggage transportation systems; cable-guided transportation systems; sliding storage systems; rail-mounted carts.

These systems demonstrate different approaches to moving objects while controlling their direction and stopping.

For example, gravity conveyors demonstrate how objects can move without a continuously powered motor. However, a school bag system cannot simply allow the bag to travel freely because the destination must be controlled.

This creates an important design requirement: Gravity can provide the movement, but a separate control mechanism is required to determine where the carrier stops.

This distinction is central to the proposed design.

1Focus and method · What went well

This is the best paragraph in the draft. You turn research into a design insight (“gravity moves it, something else must stop it”), which is what critical thinking looks like.

2Knowledge and understanding · What could be improved

The list names eight systems and then moves on. Pick three or four, say how each one controls speed and stopping (for example friction brakes, dampers, magnetic braking, detents or end-stops), and compare them in a table against your specification.

3Knowledge and understanding · What could be improved

No sources are cited. Name them, and prefer technical sources: manufacturer data sheets, standards, patents and journals, as the guide asks, over general web pages.

4Critical thinking · What could be improved

You can build the whole essay on this insight. For example, test two or three stopping mechanisms on a small rail rig and compare the stopping position and its repeatability. This gives you primary data and a clear argument.

6. Initial concept development

≈127 words

Several possible concepts were developed.

Concept A: Open sliding tray

A tray travels along a guided track beside the stairway. Advantages: simple construction; easy to load; low material requirements; easy visual access. Disadvantages: bag could fall out; limited containment; greater safety concerns.

Concept B: Enclosed carrier

The bag is placed inside a partially enclosed container attached to wheels or rollers. Advantages: improved bag security; reduced risk of objects falling out; adaptable to different bag sizes. Disadvantages: greater material use; more difficult manufacturing; increased mass.

Concept C: Hanging carrier

The bag is placed in a sling suspended from a rail-mounted carriage. Advantages: flexible for different bag shapes; potentially lightweight; suspension can reduce some vibration. Disadvantages: swinging during movement; more difficult stopping; increased risk of collision with the staircase.

1Presentation · What could be improved

There are no sketches. In DT, concepts are communicated with annotated drawings, CAD or models. Add labelled sketches or a CAD view of each concept, and number them as figures.

2Critical thinking · What could be improved

Advantages and disadvantages are claims. “Greater safety concerns” and “increased mass” need a reason or a test, such as a simple card model, a calculation of mass, or an observed swing in a quick prototype.

3Focus and method · What could be improved

All three concepts are about the carrier shape. The hard problem in your research question, stopping, is not yet a design variable. Generate concepts for how it stops (brake, damper, latch, stop-at-floor trigger) as well as for how the bag is held.

4Focus and method · What could be improved

Tense changes between “could”, “should be” and “were developed”. Decide whether this is a plan or a report and write in one tense. In the final essay everything you did is in the past tense.

1Critical thinking · What went well

The text says the matrix informs but does not decide the design. That is the right attitude, and it shows you understand a limit of the method.

2Critical thinking · What could be improved

Add the weighted totals. With these figures they come to 86 (A), 99 (B) and 78 (C), so B wins. A reader should not have to calculate that, and a short comment on how clear the margin is would be useful.

3Critical thinking · What could be improved

Test the result by changing the weights. If safety and bag security were weighted 3 instead of 5, would B still win? This sensitivity check is the kind of critical evaluation examiners reward.

4Focus and method · What could be improved

The matrix leaves out “stopping reliability” and “user effort”, which are the two key words in the research question. Use the criteria and targets from your specification so the matrix and the question line up.

5Critical thinking · What could be improved

Explain each weight and score with evidence: a survey result, a test, or a calculation. At present they look like opinion, and they are the basis for the choice of concept.

6Presentation · Check against the current rules

Keep an eye on the 4,000-word limit and the balance of the essay. A design-process write-up of this kind takes a lot of words and gives little room for analysis of evidence, and examiners give the most marks (12 of 34) for critical thinking.

How I would reshape this essay

not in word count

1. Keep the idea, change the question. Turn the design task into an investigation of the hard part. Options:

  • “To what extent does the choice of braking method (friction pad, damper or magnetic) affect the stopping-position repeatability of a gravity-driven carrier for loads of 3–8 kg?”
  • “Which of three stopping mechanisms best meets a measurable specification for a gravity-driven school bag carrier, judged by stopping tolerance, push force and pinch-point risk?”

2. Primary data. Weigh bags (as a percentage of body mass) for a sample of students, survey needs, then build a scale rail rig in the workshop and test each stopping mechanism with repeated runs. Record stopping position, speed at the stop and force to reset.

3. Secondary data. Rail and conveyor braking methods, safety guidance for moving equipment, and published data on school bag loads. Cite them.

4. Structure. Introduction and question (350), method (600), background (600), results and analysis (1,200), evaluation (600), conclusion (300).

5. Keep the design cycle, trimmed. Use the specification and concept matrix, but move most of it into a short, evidence-based section that explains the choice of mechanisms to test.

6. Check for double-dipping. If this student also has a design project (IA) about a school problem, make sure the EE uses different materials, data and focus.

1Focus and method · What went well

The rescue plan keeps what is good about the draft (the gravity-plus-control insight) and gives it an arguable question and measurable evidence.

2Focus and method · Check against the current rules

Whatever question you choose, the guide requires primary and secondary sources, an appropriate method, and the design cycle. Practical work is not compulsory, but a rig is the most direct route here.
